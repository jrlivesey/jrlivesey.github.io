Bio

I grew up mostly in Menomonie, Wisconsin, about an hour's drive east of Minneapolis. I attended Edmonds–Woodway High School in Washington state, where I completed an International Baccalaureate Diploma.

As an undergraduate at the University of Washington, I worked with Rory Barnes on constructing and applying coupled physical models of potentially habitable exoplanets, mostly through the VPLanet code. After graduation, I stayed on an extra year as a post-baccalaureate scholar. During this experience I developed a love for orbital dynamics, the thematic thread which weaves together all my published work since.

I am now a graduate student in astronomy at the University of Wisconsin–Madison, working with Juliette Becker. My current research focuses on the dynamical processes that shape the orbital architectures of exoplanetary systems over long timescales. I'm also interested in the possible environments of habitable worlds (for instance, can they form around white dwarf stars?).

In my free time I enjoy hiking, reading fiction, and hunting for the best dark ales in Madison. I also like learn about the other sciences — I minored in linguistics and still open those textbooks from time to time.

My Research

Dynamics of multiple-planet systems

Due to their high abundance in the observational sample of multi-planet systems, "peas-in-a-pod" systems are ever a subject of study and debate.

As an undergraduate and post-bac, I studied the possible dynamical histories of the Proxima Centauri planetary system. This system is of great interest to astrobiologists, as it is the closest system to our own and contains a potentially habitable world: Prox Cen b. I evaluated the orbital stability of the system across a broad parameter space in order to constrain its possible starting conditions. I also took several such stable configurations and ran simulations of the system's orbital dynamics and tidal evolution over its lifetime. The primary findings are that both inner planets must have started out with moderate orbital eccentricities, and that tidal forcing on Prox Cen b can persist for many billions of years, providing an additional energy source to heat the surface. Read the paper here.
