Bio

As an undergraduate at the University of Washington, I worked with Professor Rory Barnes on simulating the orbital dynamics of multiple-planet systems. I graduated in 2022 with a bachelor's degree in physics and astronomy, with minors in math and linguistics. In the time since, I have continued to work with Prof. Barnes and his group at UW on exoplanet modeling.

In my free time, I enjoy hiking and creative writing. Beyond science, I am also a nerd about rail transit and elections.

Research Projects

Long-term evolution of the Proxima Centauri system

Proxima b is the nearest potentially habitable exoplanet. Essential to assessing its habitability is constraining its thermal evolution. I have attempted to do this by determining plausible primordial architectures of the system. This in turn limits the degree to which Proxima b may have been tidally heated over billions of years. At right is a visualization of an N-body simulation of the inner two planets (d and b) for moderately eccentric initial orbits.

History of the L890-9 planets

Shortly after the announcement of two transiting super-Earths around LP 890-9 (SPECULOOS-2), I co-organized the 2nd Annual VPLanet Workshop. We collaborated with workshop participants to simulate the orbital, tidal, geological, and chemical history of L980-9 b and c using the modular planetary evolution code VPLanet.

Research Goals

While most of my past research has focused on orbital dynamics, I am fascinated by all aspects of planetary system evolution: from the dynamical influence of the galactic environment down to the physics of planetary interiors. Hopefully, I will soon begin graduate school. I plan to continue using semi-analytic and numerical calculations to elucidate the mechanisms that drive the long-term evolution of planets, and explore their ramifications for habitability and for bodies in our own Solar System.
